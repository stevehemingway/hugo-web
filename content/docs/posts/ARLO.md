## One-paragraph summary

Arlo sells home security cameras and charges up to about $30/month for the subscription needed to make them useful. Snowcap argues that the "high-growth, high-margin, low-churn SaaS" story Arlo tells investors is largely an artefact of (a) accounting rules that let it book a large slice of cheap camera bundle sales as subscription revenue, (b) subscriber and churn metrics dominated by low-value Verisure partnership accounts, (c) a small, quietly announced acquisition (Canary) used to buy growth, and (d) repeated KPI definition changes and disclosure withdrawals. Strip all that out, Snowcap says, and underlying subscription growth in Q2 2026 was around 1%, not the 19% reported - which would make Arlo a slow-growing hardware company masquerading as a SaaS business.

## The seven claims, in the deck's own order

### 1. Revenue stuffing via bundle accounting (slides 11-19)
- Arlo's website and Amazon carry promotions giving a **6-month Arlo Secure subscription free with a cheap camera** - bundles priced at $34.99-$64.99, sometimes below the camera-only price.
- Under ASC 606-style rules Arlo must allocate the bundle price across hardware and subscription by **relative standalone selling price**. A $34.99 bundle (camera $39.99, 6 months at $59.94) gets allocated roughly 60/40, so about **$20.99 is booked as service revenue and only $14.00 as hardware**.
- Snowcap's appendix table shows **60-69% of these bundle prices booked as subscription revenue**; the camera portion is effectively sold at a loss.
- Because most Arlo cameras ship with a **1-month free trial**, the same mechanics let Arlo book **22-33%** of a plain camera sale as service revenue. Falling ASPs (e.g. $19.98 Walmart cameras) and rising subscription prices have increased that share over time.
- The deck also flags a **FY25 change to the revenue accounting policy** giving Arlo more discretion to allocate hardware revenue to services where it deems the two interrelated.
- Consequence argued: revenue classified as high-margin recurring SaaS is actually negative-margin, one-off hardware economics - and those customers are unlikely to renew at full price.

### 2. Implausibly small hardware revenue (slides 20-28)
- Excluding bulk "cost plus" sales to Verisure (about 40% of total revenue), Snowcap estimates Arlo's **retail hardware revenue was only ~$11.5m in Q2 2026**, down **30% YoY**, even though retail point-of-sale unit volumes reportedly grew ~8-9%.
- Constant ASPs and 9% volume growth should have produced ~$16.5m. The ~$6.5m gap, Snowcap argues, is the amount pulled into service revenue.
- Sell-side is said to ascribe a **5.5x multiple to service revenue and effectively zero to hardware** - so the classification choice is worth a lot.

### 3. The churn rate is "bogus" (slides 29-34)
- Arlo claims monthly churn below 1.0% ("one of the lowest in the world").
- Snowcap estimates **72% of Arlo's paid accounts (4.08m of 5.69m) are Verisure/partnership accounts**, generating 11% of ARR at ~$0.74 ARPU versus $15.30 for retail - roughly **15 cents of gross profit per user per month** on a cost-plus contract.
- Verisure users are locked into multi-year contracts with ~0.62%/month churn (Verisure's own 7.4% annual figure). Blend 72% at 0.62% with 28% at ~2.0% and you get the headline ~1.0%.
- Cross-check: using Arlo's own disclosed retail CAC of $229 and its sales-and-marketing/gross-profit figures, Snowcap derives retail churn of **2.0-2.5%/month** (~24%/yr), versus 1.2% disclosed in 2022 - contradicting management's claim that price hikes had no churn impact.

### 4. Hidden Canary acquisition propping up growth (slides 35-42)
- In March 2026 Arlo paid **~$49-50m for Canary Connect**, disclosed only in a 10-Q footnote - no press release, no earnings-call mention. By contrast the much smaller Aloe Care deal got a release, four slides and twelve call mentions.
- Canary's ARR was last publicly put at ~$14m (Lake Street), a material chunk of the quarter's growth.
- Parallel drawn with the CFO's previous employer **CalAmp**, which ran a similar "SaaS transition" and filed for bankruptcy in 2024, two years after he left. The deck lists striking similarities: hardware stuffing, acquisitions, inflated subscriber counts.

### 5. Subscriber growth masked by KPI gymnastics (slides 43-49)
- Net adds over the target range are mostly Verisure catch-up; Arlo's **own retail subscribers grew only 8% in FY25**.
- In **Q3 2025 Arlo changed the paid-account definition** from subscriptions "being collected" to those where a fee "was collected" during the period - apparently counting anyone who paid at any point, including churned and bundled trial users. The ARR definition has been re-cut several times (Q1-23, Q1-24, Q3-25).
- In **Q2 2026 Arlo stopped disclosing ARPU**, removing the one input investors used to back out retail subscriber numbers.
- Proxy check: **credit card and app processing fees fell 11% QoQ** in Q2 while paid accounts rose 5%.

### 6. Business headwinds and competitive threat (slides 50-58)
- Reddit r/arlo is full of churn-intent posts about price hikes and lost features; Arlo has been sued over telling legacy users their cameras were end-of-life and over a "never expires" storage claim.
- Former executives say churn "significantly" ramps up as 2020-era cameras hit their **5-7 year end of life** (i.e. 2025-2027).
- **Partnership walkback:** the Q4-25 language promising deals would "accelerate momentum towards long-range targets" is replaced in Q2-26 with generic "trusted technology brand of choice" boilerplate. The ADT Blu launch (May 2026) has poor reviews and was discounted 50% within three months. Samsung and Comcast partnerships have not launched.
- Arlo **quietly scrapped its "Total Security Packages" B2B/ADT-like offering** in March 2026.
- **Eufy** (no subscription) is taking share, and **Apple's "Home Collection"** attacks precisely the premium Pro/Ultra segment that produces Arlo's best subscribers.

### 7. Illusory free cash flow and insider selling (slides 59-61)
- Arlo spends much of its FCF on buybacks to offset stock-based compensation dilution, yet **share count is up ~27% since January 2021**.
- Since 2022 the **CEO has sold ~5.6m shares and the CFO ~1.7m** - roughly 70-80% of their holdings. No open-market insider buying since November 2022.

## Valuation argument

Arlo reports **+19% YoY subscription revenue** and **+23% paid accounts** in Q2 2026. Snowcap's bridge to a ~1% underlying growth rate:

| Component (Q2 2026, YoY $m) | Adjustment |
|---|---|
| Arlo reported subscription revenue growth | +19% ($14.9m) |
| Bundle accounting impact (estimate) | -$6.5m |
| Canary acquisition (estimate) | -$3.5m to -$5m |
| Low-margin partnership revenue | -$1.5m |
| Non-recurring engineering revenue | -$1.2m |
| Remainder | ~$0.7m, i.e. **~1% YoY** |

Summary comparison table in the deck:

| Metric | Arlo reported | Snowcap estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Subscription revenue | +19% YoY | ~1% YoY |
| Subscribers | +23% YoY | ~(1)% YoY |
| Churn rate | <12%/yr, improving | ~24%/yr, deteriorating |

The punchline: low-growth hardware companies command much lower multiples, and even SaaS names de-rate sharply below 10% growth. If the 15-20% growth premise falls, the valuation support goes with it.
